# Lock the reward, never the money

Lock In should ship locked rewards as a promise the app keeps score on, not as money the app holds: the user pastes a product link, picks an attendance goal built from their own recent history, locks both, and on success the app reveals a prefilled checkout on the brand's own store that the user confirms with one tap. That version is shippable in days, needs no company, no payment code and no licence, and it avoids every failure that killed or hurt earlier products. The research supports the core idea but trims the ambitions around it. Incentives reliably raise gym attendance while they run and mostly fade afterwards, so the feature should be designed as rolling monthly cycles, not a one-off. Goals with a small visible reserve of skips beat both easy and strict goals, so "cannot change it" should mean "cannot make it easier", not "one missed day ends the month". No product found in two targeted searches combines a user-chosen real product, a locked goal and purchase on success, so the gap is real. The two things to stay away from in version 1 are holding the user's money for a month (money-transmission exposure in Washington, App Store exposure for an individual developer) and locked body-result goals (a deadline on a number that fasting and dehydration can move). Fully automatic ordering with no tap is technically reachable through buy-for-me services, but both Shopify and Stripe design their agent checkout around the buyer confirming the total, and a silent charge weeks after lock-in is the mirror image of what the FTC punished Pact for.

## Rewards raise attendance for the month, then fade, so build cycles

The strongest finding across the gym-incentive literature is that incentives work while they are live and decay when they end. In a Fortune 500 workplace study, employees responded strongly to a four-week gym incentive, but long-run effects were "modest, at best"; **the group that was then offered a self-funded commitment contract showed lasting changes detectable for years** ([NBER w18580](https://nber.org/papers/w18580)). Among 836 brand-new members of a commercial gym, cash of $30 or $60, or a $30 item the member would keep only by attending, had small effects during six weeks and **no effect on visits afterwards**, and new members were substantially overconfident about how often they would go ([NBER w23567](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23567)). A replication of the classic pay-students-to-attend study found habit "takes hold" in roughly a third of treated subjects, and that people greatly over-predict their own future attendance ([Acland & Levy](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/levymr/papers/AclandLevy.pdf)). The honest product claim is therefore "this gets you to the gym this month", and the design response is to offer the next lock at the moment of success, which is exactly where the Royer contract was offered. stickK data points the same way: users nudged toward longer exercise contracts completed more weeks (about 6 versus 3.3) and re-enrolled more often ([NBER Digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/oct15/w21406.html)).

### A reward frame gets the sign-ups; the lock supplies the bite

Loss stakes change behaviour more per person but almost nobody accepts them. In a smoking trial, **90% of people accepted a reward programme and 13.7% accepted a deposit programme**; rewards won overall, though deposits worked better among the minority willing to take them ([Halpern et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4471993)). In a voluntary deposit scheme in the Philippines about 11% signed up and 66% of those who did lost their money ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1468)). A step-count trial found a loss frame beat control (goal met on 45% of days versus 30%) while the economically identical gain frame did not ([Patel et al., PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26881417/)). Lock In's idea sits usefully between the two: it is presented as a reward, so uptake should look like the reward arm, but once a user has picked a specific hoodie and watched a counter climb toward it, missing feels like losing something that was already theirs. That borrowed loss aversion is an inference, not a tested result, and the endowment-item arm in the new-member study warns it could be small.

Theory explains why the lock is the feature and not decoration. Self-rewards only motivate if it is credible that the person will not take the reward after failing ([Koch, Nafziger, Suvorov & van de Ven](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/12/wp12_14.pdf)), and quiet goal revision is a major cause of goals not being met ([Kaiser, Koch & Nafziger](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP15716.pdf)). The app cannot physically stop anyone buying a hoodie, so its real leverage is the recorded promise, a witness, and friction; the copy should say so plainly. The earned-indulgence research adds a selection rule: effort makes people more willing to choose luxuries they would otherwise feel guilty about ([Kivetz & Zheng](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1201/Justification_and_Indulgence.pdf)). A Scuffers hoodie is a good reward; protein powder the user would have bought anyway is a bad one.

### Slack with a cost beats both easy goals and strict ones

The best-supported goal shape is a hard target with a small, visible reserve. In a step-goal field experiment, people given a seven-day goal with two "emergency skips" a week reached their goals more than people given either a five-day goal or a strict seven-day goal, and after a missed day **55% of the weekly-reserve group bounced back versus 37% of the strict group**, even though the reserve goal and the easy goal demanded the same minimum effort ([UCLA Anderson Review](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/emergency-reserves/)). The largest gym experiment to date, 54 programmes tested on 61,293 members, found the single best intervention was a tiny reward for coming back after a missed workout, worth a 27% rise in visits ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/wharton-study-best-ways-boost-workout-habits)). Rigid scheduling backfires: paying only for visits inside a pre-planned two-hour window produced fewer visits than paying for visits at any time, during and after the incentive ([Beshears et al.](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2020.3706)). Gym visits and goal commitments rise at the start of a week, month or year ([Dai, Milkman & Riis](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)), and a month-end reward is a long wait when immediate rewards predict persistence better than delayed ones ([Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/achieve-long-term-goals-focus-short-term)).

Worry about rewards killing inner motivation is mostly a lab result. The classic meta-analysis found tangible rewards undermined free-choice motivation on tasks people already found interesting ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)), but the gym replication found no crowding out ([Acland & Levy](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/levymr/papers/AclandLevy.pdf)), and field trials show attendance returning to baseline, not below it. A reward the user chooses and pays for is about as non-controlling as an incentive gets. No trial has tested this exact mechanism (a specific pre-chosen product bought only on success), so every design number below is a judgement drawn from neighbouring studies.

## Nobody ships "your own product, bought when you earn it"

The category has three business models and this idea is none of them. Loss-stake apps (stickK, Beeminder, Forfeit, and newer gym apps such as Commity) charge the user on failure. Pooled-bet products (DietBet, StepBet, HealthyWage, the defunct Pact) pay winners from losers. Sponsor-funded products (Sweatcoin, Paceline, Carrot Rewards) pay tiny rewards from someone else's budget. The pattern in their fates is clear: sponsor-funded models decay or die, Carrot Rewards shut in 2019 when funding ran out ([MobiHealthNews](https://www.mobihealthnews.com/news/canadian-wellness-incentive-app-carrot-rewards-shuts-its-doors)) and Sweatcoin reviewers complain of unavailable gift cards and negligible rewards ([Trustpilot](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/sweatco.in?page=5)), while user-funded models survive for a decade but stay small; Beeminder's founders called it profitable "but just barely" ([Beeminder blog](https://blog.beeminder.com/five)).

A search aimed specifically at "lock a product, buy it on success" returned only loss-stake apps. The nearest things are symbolic reward shops such as Habitica's custom rewards, where the user defines a real-world treat and the app buys nothing ([Habitica wiki](https://habitica.fandom.com/wiki/Rewards)); Qapital's Apple Health rules, which move money into a named savings goal per workout but have no lock, no threshold and no product ([Qapital help](https://help.qapital.com/en/articles/10245394-apple-health-rule)); and Strava's sponsored challenges, where the brand picks the reward and the user still checks out by hand. Strava's version shows the commercial pull of an "earned" purchase: brands pay from $30k to $50k per challenge, and Samsung attributed 20% of Galaxy Watch sales in one period to completion codes ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/brands-like-chipotle-duer-hoka-are-partnering-with-strava-to-make-branded-workouts/), [Strava Business](https://business.strava.com/challenges)). Small indie or non-English apps with this mechanic could exist unindexed, so the gap is "not found", not "proven empty".

The structural advantage is alignment. In every loss-stake product the operator earns when the user fails, and users notice: a Forfeit reviewer is uneasy that forfeited money goes to the app ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1633125787)). A reward model profits, if at all, only when the user succeeds. It also defuses cheating, because there is no pool or sponsor to defraud. One practical warning for the brand: a gym-stake app already trades as "Lockin" ([lockinapp.org](https://lockinapp.org/)), so App Store search will collide.

Pact is the lesson to design against. The FTC alleged it charged "tens of thousands" of consumers penalties even when they had met their goals or after they cancelled, including a service member whose base gym the app could not recognise, and buried cancellation terms; the 2017 order returned more than $940,000 to consumers and named the founders personally ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/45481), [Davis+Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/ftc-settles-with-mobile-app-developer-over-unauthorized-charges/)). Pact was not punished for the commitment idea. It was punished because a verification miss became a charge. In a design where failure costs nothing, a verification miss only delays a reward.

## The money stays in the user's bank until the tap

### A card hold cannot last a month, and holding cash is the legal line

The intuitive mechanism, a hold on the card for the price of the hoodie, does not exist for a monthly goal. Online card authorisations last **7 days** (5 for Visa merchant-initiated); the 30-day extended version requires negotiated IC+ pricing and is intended for cases where the final amount is unknown ([Stripe: holds](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/place-a-hold-on-a-payment-method), [Stripe: extended authorization](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/extended-authorization.md?platform=web&ui=elements)). Stripe's own advice when a hold does not fit is to save the payment method and charge later. The alternative, charging up front and refunding on failure, is where the legal exposure sits. Federal rules define money transmission as accepting funds from one person and transmitting them to another, and Washington's exclusion for payment facilitators requires a written contract with the payee, with the burden of proving the exclusion on the person claiming it ([31 CFR 1010.100](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100), [RCW 19.230.020](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=19.230.020), [WA DFI](https://dfi.wa.gov/money-services/licensing-frequently-asked-questions)). Whether charge-then-refund by a sole proprietor actually crosses that line is an open legal question the research could not answer; it needs a fintech lawyer before any build. Apple adds its own barrier: apps for money management should be submitted by the institution providing the service, and services in regulated fields by a legal entity, "not by an individual developer" ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).

The physical purchase itself is easy under Apple's rules. Guideline 3.1.3(e) requires physical goods to use payment methods other than in-app purchase, with no Apple commission, while 3.1.1 means any charge for the locking feature itself (a premium tier) must use in-app purchase ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Commitment-money apps such as stickK and Forfeit are live on the App Store, though no Apple statement about the category was found.

### Five rungs from a link to a real order

| Rung | What happens at success | Build effort | App touches money? | Main weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Unlock a prefilled checkout link | User taps pay on the brand's own checkout (Shop Pay or Apple Pay) | Days, no approvals | No | Nothing stops buying it early elsewhere |
| 2. Same, plus a self-managed pot | App tracks an amount the user set aside in their own bank | Days | No | Honour-based |
| 3. Card saved, order placed after a confirm tap | Charge at unlock, then a buy-for-me service orders | About a week plus a Stripe account | For hours, not a month | Card can decline; service coverage per store unproven |
| 4. Charge up front, order or refund at month end | Real "locked money" | Lawyer and ideally an LLC first | Yes, for a month | Money-transmission, refund-fee and App Review exposure |
| 5. Full agentic checkout | Agent completes checkout on a scoped token | Partnerships or seller opt-in | Token only | Not open to a solo developer today |

Rung 1 works on any Shopify store today. A cart permalink of the form `https://{shop}/cart/{variant_id}:{quantity}` can prefill email, shipping address and a discount code, and Shopify's newer `/buy/` permalink can request Shop Pay where the store's profile supports it ([Shopify: cart permalinks](https://shopify.dev/docs/apps/build/checkout/create-cart-permalinks), [Shopify: permalinks for agents](https://shopify.dev/docs/agents/carts-and-checkout/permalinks.md)). The brand is merchant of record, so returns, stock and price are its normal process, and the user sees the live price at checkout. Product details for the lock screen come from the store's public product JSON (title, image, variants, price, availability), with the caveats that some stores disable it and it omits currency ([Scrapfly](https://scrapfly.io/blog/posts/how-to-scrape-shopify-stores), [Apify](https://apify.com/makework36/shopify-products-scraper)); page-embedded product data is the fallback. These endpoints were not re-tested during this research, so they need checking against the real target stores, and because Lock In is a web view the fetch has to run in the Swift layer or a small server function to avoid cross-origin blocks.

Rung 3 is where "it just arrives" becomes real. Zinc places orders for **$1 per successful order**, enforces a required `max_price` ceiling and returns typed failures such as `out_of_stock`, but draws from a wallet the developer prefunds, which means collecting from the user first ([Zinc FAQ](https://www.zinc.com/faq)). Rye turns a product URL into a completed checkout with a `maxTotalPrice` constraint and names Shopify explicitly, but ships to US addresses only, expires each intent after 45 minutes, and does not publish pricing ([Rye docs](https://docs.commerce.jolly.dev/llm-context.md)). Two unknowns block relying on either: whether a small European streetwear store is orderable at all, and who is merchant of record. Rung 5 is closed for now: completing checkout through Shopify's protocol directly "requires Shopify's permission", and Stripe's shared payment tokens are in preview and require the seller to be onboarded ([Shopify: commerce agents](https://shopify.dev/docs/agents.md), [Stripe: shared payment tokens](https://docs.stripe.com/agentic-commerce/concepts/shared-payment-tokens?agent-seller=agent)). Even where Shopify has opened checkout to agents, the buyer must authorise the transaction before it is submitted ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/28/shopify-opens-checkout-to-browser-based-ai-agents/)).

That requirement is a feature, not an obstacle. A month's gap guarantees that prices move, sizes sell out, addresses change and people change their minds. A "You earned it. Buy now for $X?" screen absorbs all four and meets the "express informed consent" standard the Pact order set for charges.

## Failure costs nothing, so most of the legal risk disappears

**Gambling.** Washington defines gambling as staking or risking something of value on a contest of chance or an event outside the person's control, with an understanding that someone receives something of value ([RCW 9.46.0237](https://app.leg.wa.gov/rcw/default.aspx?cite=9.46.0237)). In the recommended design the user risks nothing and receives nothing from anyone else, so the question never starts. It restarts the moment money is forfeited on failure or anything is paid beyond the user's own funds (pooled pots, bonuses, prize draws), which also triggers Apple's contest and real-money gaming rules. The weight-betting companies defend themselves only with their own "you control the outcome" argument; no court or regulator ruling on it was found. Keep "bet", "wager", "stake" and "win" out of the interface and the store listing.

**Consumer protection.** Put every material term on the lock screen itself in plain words: the product, the price seen today, the goal, the skips, what counts as a visit, and the fact that failing costs nothing. Pact's cancellation terms sat beneath 4,400 words. Separate two kinds of lock. The goal and the reward cannot be made easier or swapped; the obligation to pay never exists, the user can abandon a goal at any time as a no-cost fail, and the purchase always needs a final tap. Contracts with under-18s are generally voidable (stated from general knowledge in the notes, not a retrieved source), which argues for making locked rewards an adults-only feature with a self-declared age gate.

**Privacy.** Washington's My Health My Data Act defines consumer health data broadly enough to include body measurements and health status inferred from other data, does not exempt small businesses, and makes violations actionable by private lawsuit under the Consumer Protection Act ([RCW 19.373](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=19.373&full=true)). For a Washington developer the downside is lopsided, so gym-visit logs, weight and photos should be treated as covered and kept on the device: no server copy, no iCloud sync of health data (Apple forbids storing personal health information in iCloud), no analytics events carrying location, no photos sent to a cloud model without separate opt-in consent. Whether purely on-device processing counts as "collection" by the developer is untested, so publishing a short consumer health data privacy policy is cheap insurance. A geofence around the user's own gym is probably outside the Act's ban on geofencing health-care facilities, but that is an inference and some gyms share buildings with clinics.

**Body-result goals.** These are the one part of the original idea to cut from version 1. Weight incentives produce short-term loss that is largely regained: a deposit-contract trial saw about 14 lb lost versus 3.9 lb at 16 weeks, not sustained at seven months ([Volpp et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3583583)), and a 2026 meta-analysis found the effect disappears once payments stop ([Obesity Reviews](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/obr.70124)). A locked deadline on a scale number has the shape of a weigh-in, which is why the companies that live on weight goals ban purging, dehydration and starvation, cap the rate of loss, exclude pregnancy and require adults ([HealthyWage rules](https://www.healthywage.com/rules/official-rules/)), and why DietBet sets a BMI floor of 18.5 ([DietBet rules](https://waybetter.app/dietbet/rules)). A study of 493 college students found diet and fitness app users reported higher eating-disorder symptoms ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8485346)). No study directly measures eating-disorder harm among weight-wager users, so the risk is inferred, but body weight is also only partly under the user's control, which weakens the gambling analysis under Washington's "material degree" chance test, and Apple bars apps from urging challenges that risk physical harm. If body goals arrive later they should be opt-in, adults only, blocked below a BMI of 18.5, capped at the CDC's gradual pace of about 1 to 2 lb a week ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-weight-growth/losing-weight/index.html)), judged on a multi-week trend and not one weigh-in, and droppable at any time.

**Verification.** Because the only person a cheat can rob is themselves, the aim is to make self-deception slightly effortful, not to build fraud detection. The error that actually damages trust is the missed real visit. A geofence only tests whether a reported coordinate is inside a boundary, and location spoofing tools for iPhone are public ([GPS-Spoof](https://github.com/Lukejb14/GPS-Spoof)); attendance-software vendors recommend layering signals and reviewing flagged check-ins instead of auto-rejecting them, since GPS drift flags honest users ([BazaTracker](https://bazattracker.com/blog/gps-attendance/how-to-stop-fake-gps-attendance), a vendor source). Forfeit's menu shows the category standard: GPS check-in, AI-checked photo, Apple Health thresholds, friend verification and human appeals ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/id1633125787)). Apple also requires an alternative when a user declines a permission, so manual check-in has to exist anyway.

## Version 1 is a promise, a counter and a checkout link

Version 1 should do the following, all of it on the device and none of it touching money. The user pastes a product link; the app pulls the name, picture, size options and today's price, and stores the exact variant. The goal is attendance only, for one calendar month, and the lock is offered to start on the 1st or a Monday. The app suggests a target at roughly 120% of the user's own recent visits, the level used in the reserve study, and warns when the chosen number is far above their history; a user averaging six visits who picks twenty is being set up to fail. The target is shown as a weekly rhythm with a declared reserve, for example "20 this month, 2 passes". A 24-hour cooling-off window lets the user fix an overconfident target, after which the reward, the target and the gym location cannot be made easier; raising the target stays allowed. An injury or illness pause freezes the clock and extends the deadline, limited to one or two uses per cycle, so no one has to choose between training hurt and losing the hoodie. The window length and pause limits are judgement calls; no study tests them.

A visit counts on any time of day when the phone has stayed at the saved gym for a set dwell time, or a device-recorded workout overlaps, or the user takes a live in-app photo, which fits the camera flow Lock In already has. Any one signal is enough in version 1, and a small number of honour-based "I was there, the app missed it" claims per month cover the rest. Every visit moves a progress picture toward the product, and a miss triggers a recovery message showing the goal is still reachable, the megastudy's best-performing moment. If the goal becomes mathematically impossible, a fallback declared at lock time (the same locked item rolls into next month) avoids the cliff where users quit. On success the app shows the confirm screen, re-checks price and stock, and opens the prefilled checkout; if the item is gone or dearer, the user chooses another or walks away. The success screen then offers the next lock. Failure costs nothing and is recorded in a visible history, since the record is where the commitment's credibility comes from.

The roadmap adds one risk at a time. Version 1.1 adds a friend as witness: stickK weight-loss contracts with both stakes and a referee succeeded 61.3% of the time versus 14.5% with neither ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5316505)), and a progress card ("14/20 toward the hoodie") is shareable in a way a forfeit is not; sharing visit data with a friend needs its own opt-in. Version 1.2 adds the self-managed pot and stricter optional verification (two signals out of three). Version 2 tests a saved card with a confirm tap and a buy-for-me order, starting with one or two stores confirmed orderable, after a short legal review of the consent screens and with a Stripe account; an LLC is sensible by this point. Brand discounts or affiliate commission on the locked item are a plausible revenue line, but the research found no usable affiliate sources and the new `/buy/` links cannot carry referral codes, so this is unproven. Held funds, forfeits, pooled pots and locked body goals stay out until a lawyer has reviewed them, and the first three do not look worth it for a solo developer at all.

## Conclusion

The research reverses the instinct behind the idea. The feeling that the money is "locked away" seems like the heart of the feature, but the evidence says the motivating part is the specific object, the recorded promise and the visible count, while the locked cash is what brings licensing questions, refund fees and the Pact failure mode. Dropping custody costs little motivation and removes most of the risk, and dropping the forfeit turns verification from a billing system into a scoreboard, which is why light, forgiving checks are enough.

Two things remain unknown and are worth measuring from the first cohort. No study has tested a pre-chosen product as a reward, so Lock In's own data on completion rates by target difficulty and reserve size will be the best evidence available anywhere. And because one incentive cycle rarely builds a habit, the number that decides whether this is a feature or a gimmick is how many users lock a second reward after the first one arrives.
